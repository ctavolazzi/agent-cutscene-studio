# YouTube packaging

## Checklist

1. Upload the 4K master (`out/<name>-4k.mp4`): YouTube gives 4K uploads a much better encode, which matters for
   pixel art.
2. Thumbnail: `packaging/thumbnail.jpg` (1280x720, under 2 MB), checked at 320 px wide. Keep 2 or 3 candidates for
   YouTube's thumbnail test.
3. Title: the film's name plus what it is ("A PS1-style cutscene written entirely by AI").
4. Description: the hook in the first two lines (they show above "more"), how it was made, chapters, tools, three
   hashtags (the first three show above the title).
5. Chapters: the first at 0:00, at least three, each at least 10 seconds long. Group short shots.
6. No dashes in any of it (the director's rule).
7. Link the code: `https://github.com/ctavolazzi/agent-cutscene-studio/tree/main/films/<slug>`.

## Prompt

```
Write the YouTube title, description and chapters for films/[slug] in agent-cutscene-studio. Read its README.md,
BUILD_LOG.md and the shot list in src/shots.js. Write in my voice (I directed, Claude Code built it). Use only numbers
that are in the build log. Chapters at least 10 s each, first at 0:00. No em dashes or en dashes. End with the repo
link and three hashtags. Use playbook/prompts/youtube.md (Snow, Lamp, Bridge) as the model.
```

## Example: Snow, Lamp, Bridge

```
A silent 112-second night scene in the style of a PlayStation 1 cutscene: a street lamp in falling snow, a car that pulls up, two people who walk onto a bridge, light a cigarette and watch the moon rise. Every pixel of it was made from code written by an AI.

I made this with Claude Code, Anthropic's AI coding agent. I gave it the idea and made the calls at each review stop. Claude planned the shots, wrote the code, built the scene, rendered it and checked its own work. No 3D models, textures or footage were imported: everything you see is generated by code.

Watch in 4K for the sharpest pixels. There is no sound, on purpose.

HOW IT WAS MADE

1. Plan and storyboard. 15 shots on one 112-second clock, each with a lens, a camera move and a story beat. A top-down map of the street, the bridge and every camera position kept the 180 degree rule straight before anything was built.

2. Blockout. Grey boxes first: the street, the bridge, the car and two jointed figures. Every frame is a pure function of time with seeded randomness, so any single frame can be rendered on its own and comes out identical every time.

3. Review rounds. Stills of every shot were checked, and the problems went to me as decisions: a car tyre filling a fifth of the frame, a lamp post growing out of someone's head, a hop instead of a step out of the car. The fixes went back in.

4. The PS1 look. The scene renders at 480x270 and is scaled up 4x with hard pixels. Vertices snap to the pixel grid (the PS1's famous jitter), textures bend because they are mapped without perspective correction (its affine warp), and colour is cut to 5 bits per channel with a 4x4 dither, close to the console's 15-bit colour.

5. Light and weather. Seven lights in all: the lamp, two headlights, the moon, the lighter, the cigarette ember and the night sky. Twelve thousand snowflakes catch the lamp light and the headlight beams. The glows are additive sprites and the light shafts are soft cones.

6. Details that come from the animation. Footprints are found by watching the walk: wherever a boot comes down in the snow, a print appears at that moment. The car presses a tyre track into the snow under each wheel as it drives in. The flame, ember and smoke follow the same timing as their lights.

7. A camera move found by search. In "Feet in the snow" the camera had to turn from the car door toward the bridge without a wheel filling the frame, keep the walking feet in shot, and catch both headlight beams before they switch off. A search over hundreds of possible camera paths found the move.

8. Render and check. Each frame was rendered in a headless browser and piped into ffmpeg. 85 automated tests, a determinism check and a comparison of frames from the finished video against fresh renders confirmed the video shows exactly what the code draws.

CHAPTERS

```
